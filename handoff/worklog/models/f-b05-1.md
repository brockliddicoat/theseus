## MODEL (M, model auditor), 2026-11-06 08:45: the 2D plane-against-triangles model (shlHot) and the specific-strength ranking

Scripts that reproduce every number below: /home/claude/work/models/tests/ (t-shlhot.js, t-work.js, t-hot-buckle.js).
The solver itself (07-sim.js) is sound: 35 textbook checks pass (cantilever PL³/3EI, simply supported PL³/48EI,
fixed-fixed, axial, inclined bars, the pin-jointed triangle and square). The problems are in how shlHot reads it and in
what the slides say it means.

**M-b05-1 (must, b05_nopick, b05_hotspot, b05_margin, b05_work): the peak is sampled 6 % low.** shlHot sets
`r.peak = samples[0].s`, and shlSamples' outermost row sits at 15/32 of the depth, not at the surface. Beam theory for the
plane (M = PL/4 = 150 N·mm, Z = wH²/6 = 7.90 mm³) gives 18.99 MPa at 10 N; the solver's own `r.smax` agrees; `r.peak` is
17.79 MPa. Fix in b05_fr(): use the solver's fibre peak, `crack = r => SHL_STR.ft / r.smax * 10` and
`out.same = R.sized.smax / R.plate.smax`. Effect: the plane cracks at **63 N** (not 67), the sized triangles at **121 N**
(not 126), the ratio is **1.9×** (1.91; it printed 1.8× from 1.86), the same press at **−47 %** weight (not −46 %); the
one-size triangles stay at 90 N.

**M-b05-2 (must, b05_nopick): "cracks at 67 N, about the hardest a hand can squeeze" is not something this model can
say, and the deck's own 3D study says the opposite.** The 2D crack force is set by the section's arbitrary size: 0.28 g,
60 × 10.4 mm, 0.44 mm thick out of the page (thinner than the 0.89 mm molding floor b05_wall uses). Make it twice as
thick and it cracks at twice the force. The 3D study of the whole shell (3d/lattice/assets/lattice.json, fe.summary; the
same numbers as SHL_FE) has the plain 15.18 g wall at 3.84 MPa (P99) under a 10 N crown press and 4.14 MPa under a 10 N
squeeze: a hand's 72 N squeeze puts it near 30 MPa, a quarter of 120 MPa. And in that study the squeeze reverses the 2D
result: triangle ribs 5.55 MPa against the plain wall's 4.14 MPa (+34 %); only the crown press favours them (3.39
against 3.84, −12 %). Fix: drop the hand-squeeze tie from the plane's crack force (keep human scale where it is true:
the press the finger makes, "flex drawn 10× real"), say on the slide that the parts are a 0.28 g section and this is the
principle in 2D, and claim nothing about a plain-walled mouse cracking in the hand.

**M-b05-3 (must, b05_nopick, and b05_hotspot / b05_work if they reuse the labels): name the parts for what the model
is.** (a) The "plain solid wall" is a deep web loaded in its own plane (a 10.4 mm deep, 0.44 mm thick strip pressed on its
top edge); b05_material's strip, one slide earlier, is a wall pressed on its face. Two different mechanics under one name
will confuse the careful viewer. Its win is partly the I-beam effect (material moved out to the chords), which is real
and teachable. Call it "a solid web" or "a solid section". (b) "Theseus: triangles sized to their force" is a fully stressed
truss (member depths 0.43 to 2.10 mm, each set by its own force); Theseus's lattice is ribs of one size (the 3D study's).
The same model's one-size triangles take **90 N, 1.4×, or the same press at −29 % weight**. Either put Theseus's name on
the one-size triangles, or call the blue line "triangles sized to their force" and say Theseus's graded walls (#93) move
toward it. Checked and fine: in-plane buckling (a linear buckling analysis of the frames: 175 N one size, 193 N sized,
both above their crack loads).

**M-b05-4 (must, b05_work): 18, 25 and 90 % need the true peak and their threshold on screen.** Against the true peak the
shares stressed to at least half the part's own peak are **15, 24 and 87 %** (the closed form for the plane, a beam under a
central load, is 0.5 − 0.5 ln 2 = 15.3 %: it matches). The shares swing with the threshold: at 30 % of the peak they are
34, 87 and 99 % (the one-size triangles leap from 24 to 87). So the slide must print the rule ("stressed to at least half
its own peak"), or use a figure with no threshold: the mean stress as a share of the peak, **25, 42 and 56 %**. Don't use
07-sim.js's `feEngaged` for this: it counts a whole element as working when one end's outer fibre passes 30 %, which gives
70, 90 and 100 %. And don't carry the old shlWork's "≈ X g of the 15.2 g stress study": it multiplies a 2D frame's share by
the 3D study's body weight, two different models.

**M-b05-5 (should, b05_specific): say "bent, at one weight" in the headline.** The strip demonstration is right (a thin
wall at a fixed weight in bending ranks by E^⅓/ρ and σ^½/ρ: carbon PP 1st in stiffness, 2nd in strength behind FR4, all
numbers on the slide reproduce: 0.606 g, magnesium 0.558 mm, cracks at 8.0 N, PP good for 13.3 N, 1.7× the sag, 1.8× the
weight at the same size). But the textbook "specific stiffness" E/ρ and "specific strength" σ/ρ, the words in the title,
rank carbon PP **3rd** (aluminium 25.5, magnesium 24.8, PP 13.4 GPa per g/cm³; FR4 232, magnesium 127, PP 119 MPa per
g/cm³). "4th → 1st, per gram" without "bent, at one weight" reads as false to anyone who knows E/ρ. Label the verdict
"Stiffness per gram, as a thin wall {(1st is best)}" (or similar) and keep the caption's "a shell has a weight to hit".
Also: magnesium's 230 MPa is its ultimate strength; it yields (bends for good) at 150 MPa, 5.2 N on this strip. The
comment says so; the slide says "cracks at 8 N". Cast AZ91D is brittle enough that this is fair, but "gives at 5 N, cracks
at 8 N" would be the fully honest pair.
